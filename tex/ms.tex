\begin{abstract}
Optical spectrophotometry opens a wide window into the physical
conditions, dynamical state, and formation history of galaxies and
quasars. \fastspecfit{} is open-source Python software that extracts
this information by jointly modeling each DESI spectrum and its
broadband photometry using physically motivated stellar continuum and
emission-line templates. Measured quantities include light-weighted
stellar ages, velocity dispersions, stellar masses, star-formation
rates, K-corrections, rest-frame absolute magnitudes and colors, and
integrated fluxes and equivalent widths for more than 40 emission
lines spanning the rest-frame UV through near-infrared. We describe
the algorithms, validate the measurements using repeat observations
and comparisons to complementary codes, and present the \fastspecfit{}
value-added catalog for the DESI Data Release 2, comprising \todo{N}
million extragalactic targets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument \citep[DESI;][]{levi13a,
  desi-collaboration16a, desi-collaboration24e} is conducting an
eight-year survey (2021--2029) which will ultimately yield
spectroscopic redshifts for more than 60 million galaxies and quasars
over 17,000~deg$^2$. These data are being made public in a series of
staged releases. The Early Data Release
\citep[EDR;][]{desi-collaboration24d}, comprising 1.7~million unique
spectra from the survey validation (SV) campaign (2020 December--2021
May), was released in 2023 June. Data Release~1
\citep[DR1;][]{desi-collaboration26a}, released in 2025 March,
uniformly reprocessed the SV data and added the first 13 months of the
main survey, for a total of 20.4~million unique spectra. Data
Release~2 (DR2), the subject of this paper, extends the main-survey
data through 2024 April, its first three years, includes a staggering
37 million unique redshifts, and is scheduled for public release in
the first part of 2027. Each successive release has sharpened DESI's
baryon acoustic oscillation distance measurements to sub-percent
precision and currently shows a 2.8--4.2$\sigma$ preference for an
evolving dark energy equation of state over the standard $\Lambda$CDM
concordance cosmological model \citep{adame25b, adame25d, adame25e,
  abdul-karim25a, abdul-karim25b}.

Each spectrum DESI acquires encodes a wealth of astrophysical
information beyond its redshift. The shape and absorption-line content
of the stellar continuum trace the underlying stellar populations,
dust content, and kinematics of a galaxy, while its emission-line
spectrum constrains the star-formation rate, chemical abundances,
ionization state, and gas kinematics, as well as the presence and
strength of an active galactic nucleus (AGN) \citep[and references
  therein]{conroy13a}. Extracting these physical properties at DESI
scale---and combining them with broadband photometry to anchor the
overall spectral energy distribution---requires a fitting code that is
fast, robust, and automated across an enormous range of
signal-to-noise ratios, redshifts, and galaxy types.

A variety of publicly available codes already perform this kind of
spectrophotometric modeling. Full-spectrum fitting codes---among them
\texttt{pPXF} \citep{cappellari04a, cappellari23a}, \texttt{GandALF}
\citep{sarzi06a, sarzi17a}, \texttt{STARLIGHT}
\citep{cid-fernandes05a}, \texttt{FIREFLY} \citep{wilkinson17a},
\texttt{FIT3D}/\texttt{pyPipe3D} \citep{sanchez16a, sanchez22a},
\texttt{ULySS} \citep{koleva09a}, \texttt{alf} \citep{conroy18a}, and
others---extract the kinematics and properties of the stellar
populations and line-emitting gas; spectral energy distribution
fitting codes such as \texttt{CIGALE} \citep{boquien19a},
\texttt{Bagpipes} \citep{carnall18a}, \texttt{Prospector}
\citep{johnson21a}, and \texttt{MAGPHYS} \citep{da-cunha08a} combine
photometry (and, in some cases, spectroscopy) with physically
motivated stellar population synthesis (SPS) models to infer
star-formation histories, stellar masses, and dust properties; and
codes like \texttt{PyQSOFit} \citep{guo18a} are tailored to fitting
the emission-line spectra of quasars.

Each of these codes makes different trade-offs among flexibility,
physical realism, and computational speed, but none were designed to
be run efficiently on the tens of millions of spectra acquired by
DESI. Existing codes also do not take advantage of the DESI resolution
matrix, which guarantees uncorrelated flux uncertainties from pixel to
pixel \citep{bolton10a, guy23a}; instead, they generally resample
spectra onto a common wavelength grid or into the rest frame, which
violates one of the core assumptions underpinning maximum-likelihood
fitting by introducing off-diagonal terms into the pixel covariance
matrix.

\fastspecfit\footnote{\url{https://fastspecfit.readthedocs.io}} has
been developed to meet both these challenges, scaling efficiently to
tens of millions of spectra while fully exploiting DESI's native,
optimally extracted spectral resolution. Using a forward-modeling
fitting approach, \fastspecfit{} convolves stellar continuum and
emission-line templates with each fiber's resolution matrix and
compares the result directly to the observed spectra, preserving the
uncorrelated pixel statistics that resampling would otherwise destroy
(\S\S\ref{ssec:continuum}-\ref{ssec:emlines}). In addition,
\fastspecfit{} is engineered for speed; it fits a typical DESI
spectrum in a fraction of a second on a single CPU core, making it
possible to process the full DESI dataset---and to refit it as the
data-reduction pipeline and SPS models evolve---on modest computing
resources. This speed also makes it practical to estimate parameter
uncertainties from repeated fits to Monte Carlo-resampled data rather
than from computationally expensive posterior sampling
(\S\ref{ssec:uncertainties}), and, via a hybrid message passing
interface (MPI) plus multiprocessing design, to scale from a single
serial process on a laptop to thousands of cores on a supercomputer
(\S\ref{ssec:performance}).

Beyond speed, \fastspecfit{} is designed to be flexible, applying only
the physical constraints the data strictly require and otherwise
letting the data determine the best-fitting model. The continuum model
imposes only minimal priors: non-negative stellar population
coefficients, an energy-conserving dust model, and a freely fitted
stellar velocity dispersion bounded widely enough to accommodate the
full spectral diversity of DESI targets. The emission-line model is
similarly flexible, supporting independent narrow and broad kinematic
components and capturing the broad Balmer-line emission that
distinguishes type~1 AGN whenever the data require it. Unlike codes
that target a specific class or spectral type, \fastspecfit{} applies
this same machinery uniformly to every spectrum, without assuming in
advance whether the target is quiescent, star-forming, a
post-starburst, or hosts an AGN. This flexibility minimizes
classification-driven selection effects and maximizes the discovery
potential of a dataset as large and diverse as DESI, where unusual or
physically ambiguous objects emerge naturally from the fit rather than
requiring special-case handling.

Although this paper presents the first comprehensive description of
\fastspecfit{}'s algorithms, measurements from the earlier EDR and DR1
value-added catalogs (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_vacs}) have already
enabled a remarkably broad range of extragalactic science by both DESI
collaboration members and the broader scientific community. We briefly
highlight a small, illustrative sample of that work
here. Emission-line fluxes, equivalent widths, and stellar masses from
\fastspecfit{} have been used to identify and characterize
changing-look AGN \citep{guo25a} and to triple the census of dwarf AGN
candidates in the local universe \citep{pucha25a}; to measure
direct-method ($T_e$-based) chemical abundances for nearly 50,000
star-forming galaxies in DESI DR2 \citep{scholte26a}, including two of
the most metal-poor star-forming galaxies ever found (J.~Moustakas
et~al., in preparation), and to characterize the atomic gas sequence
and mass-metallicity relation across five decades in stellar mass
\citep{scholte24a}; to assemble catalogs of post-starburst galaxies
\citep{soto25a} and cosmic voids \citep{rincon25a}; and to measure
stellar velocity dispersions for strong gravitational lens candidates
\citep{huang25a}. The \fastspecfit{} measurements have also been
essential to measurements of the galaxy luminosity function
\citep{moore26a} and stellar mass functions \citep{wang25a}, and to
the modeling of intrinsic alignments \citep{siegel25a} and
photometric-redshift calibration \citep{blanco25a} for the next
generation of weak-lensing surveys.

In this paper we describe the DESI spectroscopic and photometric data
(\S\ref{sec:data}) and the algorithms underlying \fastspecfit{} in
detail, including the spectrophotometric and physical quantities the
code derives (\S\ref{sec:modeling}), and illustrate the complete
fitting sequence applied end-to-end to three representative targets
spanning the range of DESI extragalactic classes
(\S\ref{sec:casestudy}).  We then present the \fastspecfit{}
value-added catalog for DESI DR2, comprising 37 million extragalactic
targets, along with updated catalogs for the Early Data Release and
Data Release 1, document the code's computational performance, and
validate the measurements against independent codes
(\S\ref{sec:vac}). We conclude with a summary and an outlook toward
future improvements (\S\ref{sec:summary}).

Throughout this paper we adopt a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology with
$\Omega_m = 0.315$, consistent with \citet{planck-collaboration20a},
and a \citet{chabrier03a} initial mass function (IMF) spanning
$0.1$--$100$~\msun; we define $h \equiv H_0 / (100~\kms\,{\rm
Mpc}^{-1})$ and express stellar masses and other derived quantities in
units with $h=1$.

\section{Spectroscopic and Photometric Data}
\label{sec:data}
%% ============================================================

In this section we describe the DESI spectroscopic and broadband
photometric data that \fastspecfit{} fits (\S\ref{ssec:spectra},
\S\ref{ssec:photometry}), and the target classes and redshift
measurements that define the parent sample (\S\ref{ssec:targets}).

\subsection{DESI Spectroscopy}
\label{ssec:spectra}

DESI is a highly multiplexed fiber spectrograph mounted on the Mayall
4-m telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory
\citep{desi-collaboration16b, abareshi22a}.  A wide-field optical
corrector \citep{miller24a} feeds a $3\fdg2$-diameter field of view,
within which 5000 robotically actuated, $1\farcs5$-diameter fibers
\citep{poppett24a} are positioned onto target coordinates and feed ten
three-arm spectrographs covering 3600--9824\,\AA\ in the observed
frame \citep{guy23a}. The focal plane is divided into ten wedge-shaped
\emph{petals}, each read out by one spectrograph, and a single
pointing of the full 5000-fiber focal plane defines a \emph{tile}, the
basic unit of the survey's tiling strategy \citep{schlafly23a}. The
three cameras sense blue (B: 3600--5930\,\AA), red (R:
5600--7720\,\AA), and near-infrared (Z: 7470--9824\,\AA) light, at a
spectral resolution of $\mathcal{R}\equiv\lambda/\Delta\lambda
2000-3500$, $3400-4800$, and $3800-5200$, respectively \citep{guy23a}.

The spectroscopic pipeline \citep{guy23a} performs an optimal spectral
extraction \citep[following][]{bolton10a}, encoding the
wavelength-dependent line-spread function (LSF) for each fiber in a
sparse resolution matrix, which \fastspecfit{} uses to convolve model
spectra before comparing them to the data (see \S\ref{sec:modeling});
the resulting one-dimensional spectra are flux-calibrated,
sky-subtracted, and resampled onto a common linear wavelength grid, in
vacuum, with 0.8~\AA{} pixels.

Wavelength calibration proceeds in three steps: an initial solution
from arc-lamp spectra, a per-exposure point-spread function (PSF) and
trace refinement, and a final adjustment via cross-correlation with
sky emission lines and an external high-resolution sky atlas. The
residual wavelength calibration uncertainty after this correction is
typically $\epsilon_\lambda \approx 0.025$\,\AA\ per sky
line---roughly 3\% of a CCD pixel, or $\sim$1\,\kms\ at
7000\,\AA\ \citep{guy23a}. This precision is independently
corroborated by repeat radial-velocity measurements of stars in the
DR1 stellar Value-Added Catalog, which find a systematic velocity
floor of 1.0\,\kms\ (0.65\,\kms\ for exposures obtained on the same
night) for the bright program (\S\ref{ssec:targets}) \citep{koposov25a}.

Flux calibration uses approximately ten F-type standard stars per
spectrograph petal, selected on the basis of their $g-r$ and $r-z$
colors \citep{guy23a}. A stellar spectral model is fit to each
standard-star spectrum, and the ratio of observed to model flux,
averaged over all standard-star fibers in a camera, yields a mean
wavelength-dependent throughput correction; consistency between petals
is enforced upstream, during fiber flat-fielding, by combining the
fiber flats from all ten spectrographs of a given camera and
normalizing them to a common mean spectrum, effectively
inter-calibrating the petals before the flux-calibration vector is
derived \citep{guy23a}. Comparison of the calibrated spectra against
hydrogen-atmosphere white dwarf models shows that the residual flux
calibration error is about $2\%$ over most of the
3600--9800\,\AA\ range, rising to $\sim6\%$ shortward of 3700\,\AA;
the scatter between spectroscopically and photometrically (Legacy
Survey) determined broadband fluxes is comparably $\pm6\%$ for bright
stars \citep{guy23a}.

\subsection{Broadband Photometry}
\label{ssec:photometry}

\fastspecfit{} uses broadband photometry from the DESI Legacy Imaging
Surveys Data Release 9 (DR9) \citep[hereafter, Legacy
  Surveys;][]{dey19a}, which cover the full DESI footprint using two
imaging surveys and three cameras. South of a declination boundary of
approximately $+32\arcdeg$, $grz$ imaging comes from the DECam Legacy
Survey \citep[DECaLS;][]{dey19a}, obtained with the Dark Energy Camera
\citep[DECam;][]{flaugher15a} on the Blanco 4-m telescope at the Cerro
Tololo Inter-American Observatory (CTIO).  North of this boundary,
$g$- and $r$-band imaging comes from the Beijing-Arizona Sky Survey
\citep[BASS;][]{zou17a}, obtained with the 90Prime camera
\citep{williams04a} on the Bok 2.3-m telescope, and $z$-band imaging
comes from the Mayall $z$-band Legacy Survey (MzLS), obtained with the
Mosaic-3 camera \citep{dey16a} on the Mayall 4-m telescope; both
telescopes are sited at Kitt Peak National Observatory
(KPNO). Following \citet{myers23a}, we track the imaging system for
each target via the \texttt{PHOTSYS} flag (\texttt{N} or \texttt{S})
to assign the appropriate filter curves.

The Legacy Survey catalogs are generated using the Tractor
forward-modeling photometry pipeline \citep{lang16a, lang16b}, which
fits a common source model simultaneously across all available imaging
to derive the integrated flux for each source. The catalogs also
include forced photometry in all four WISE bands---$W1$
(3.4\,\micron), $W2$ (4.6\,\micron), $W3$ (12\,\micron), and $W4$
(22\,\micron)---derived from unWISE coadds of the full WISE and
NEOWISE mission imaging \citep{wright10a, lang14a,
  meisner21a}. \fastspecfit{} fits this total photometry, in all seven
$grz$+WISE bands, together with the DESI spectrum in a joint
spectrophotometric fit (\S\ref{sec:modeling}); in any band where a
source is undetected, the $1\sigma$ flux uncertainty is used as an
upper limit.

\subsection{DESI Target Classes and Redshifts}
\label{ssec:targets}

This section briefly summarizes the DESI survey and program structure,
the extragalactic target classes, and the redshift-determination
pipeline needed to interpret the \fastspecfit{} value-added catalogs;
for a complete description see \citet{desi-collaboration24e,
  desi-collaboration24d, desi-collaboration26a}.

DESI observations are organized by survey and program
\citep{schlafly23a, myers23a}. The primary five-year program is the
main survey, which began on 2021 May 14 and was preceded by several SV
phases, most notably the One-Percent Survey (SV3), which covered
roughly 1\% of the final DESI footprint to high spectroscopic
completeness \citep{desi-collaboration24e,
  desi-collaboration24d}. \todo{Need to reconcile this statement with
  the eight-year program mentioned in the introduction.} Within each
survey, observations are assigned to the bright program (moonlight or
reduced transparency), the dark program (nominal dark-sky conditions),
or the backup program \citep[twilight and poor-weather
  time;][]{dey26a}, on the basis of sky conditions.

DESI photometrically selects primary extragalactic targets from the
Legacy Surveys \citep{dey19a} and organizes them into four classes
spanning complementary redshift ranges \citep{myers23a}. The bright
galaxy survey \citep[BGS, $0 < z \lesssim 0.6$;][]{hahn23a} targets
galaxies to $r \lesssim 20.2$~mag during bright conditions; the
luminous red galaxy \citep[LRG, $0.4 < z \lesssim 1.1$;][]{zhou23a},
emission-line galaxy \citep[ELG, $0.6 < z \lesssim
  1.6$;][]{raichoor23a}, and quasar \citep[QSO, $0.9 < z \lesssim
  4$;][]{chaussidon23a} samples are all observed under dark
conditions; and finally secondary or tertiary (special) targets are
observed as part of a wide range of bespoke science programs and can
be assigned to any program \citep{myers23a, desi-collaboration26a}.

Spectroscopic redshifts and spectral classifications are determined by
Redrock (S.~Bailey et~al., in preparation; \citealt{anand24a}), a
principal component analysis (PCA) template-fitting code that
independently fits templates for stars, galaxies, and quasars over a
type-dependent grid of trial redshifts (S.~Bailey et al., in
preparation; \citealt{guy23a, anand24a}). The two primary
redshift-quality diagnostics reported by Redrock are \texttt{ZWARN}, a
per-object warning bitmask where \texttt{ZWARN=0} signals no known
hardware, data-quality, or redshift-fitting problems, and
\texttt{DELTACHI2}, the difference in $\chi^2$ between the best and
second-best fits across all spectral classes. Three after-burner
processing steps follow Redrock: an emission-line fitter
(\texttt{emlinefit}; \citealt{raichoor23a}) used to validate ELG
redshifts; a Mg\,\textsc{ii}~$\lambda\lambda$2796,2803 doublet fitter
\citep{chaussidon23a}; and the \texttt{QuasarNET} neural-network QSO
classifier \citep{busca18a, green25a}. The latter two after-burners
are essential for correctly classifying and measuring accurate
redshifts for QSOs (see \S\ref{ssec:zqso}).

\section{Spectrophotometric Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}
%% ============================================================

This section describes the technical, algorithmic structure of
\fastspec, the default spectrophotometric fitting mode within the
larger \fastspecfit{} software package. Readers interested in worked
examples of \fastspec{} applied to individual DESI targets may safely
skip to \S\ref{sec:casestudy}, while those interested only in the
resulting DR2 value-added catalog may skip to \S\ref{sec:vac}.

Let $F(\lambda;\theta)$ be the observed-frame UV-through-IR spectral
energy distribution (SED) of a galaxy, where $\theta$ denotes the
physical properties---stellar mass, star-formation history, dust
content, kinematics, redshift, and so on---that determine it.
%In practice, DESI fiber spectroscopy captures only $f(\lambda;\theta)
%\equiv F(\lambda;\theta)/\eta$, where $\eta$ is the
%aperture-correction factor relating the flux enclosed within the fiber
%to the galaxy's total flux (generally $\eta \gtrsim 1$, although in
%practice it can scatter below unity; see \S\ref{sssec:apercorr}). We
%rescale the observed fiber spectrum by $\eta$ before fitting, so that
%our data---the spectral pixels $d_i$ and broadband photometry $D_k$
%(\S\ref{sssec:fitting})---are on the same total-flux scale as $F$.
Our overarching goal is to find the model
\begin{equation}
  M(\lambda;\theta) = C(\lambda;\theta_C) + E(\lambda;\theta_E),
  \label{eq:totmodel}
\end{equation}
the sum of a \emph{stellar continuum model} $C$ (\S\ref{ssec:continuum})
and an \emph{emission-line model} $E$ (\S\ref{ssec:emlines}), that best
reproduces $F$ in a maximum-likelihood sense, where $\theta \equiv
\theta_C \cup \theta_E$ is the union of the continuum and
emission-line parameter sets.

\input{tables/table-notation}

A key algorithmic choice we make in \fastspec{} is to fit $C$ and $E$
sequentially rather than jointly, subtracting the best-fitting
continuum model from the data before fitting the emission lines
(\S\ref{ssec:emlines}). We adopt this two-stage design primarily for
flexibility: because $C$ and $E$ draw on different spectral
information---the overall continuum shape and broadband photometry for
$C$, (relatively) narrow ``residual'' features for $E$---decoupling
them lets us fit the lines robustly even when the continuum model
itself is unreliable, as when quasar continua are poorly represented
by our SPS templates (\S\ref{ssec:templates}) or calibration
systematics leave residual continuum-level errors
(\S\ref{sssec:smooth}).

We begin by describing the fitting pipeline's two primary model
inputs, the SPS template library (\S\ref{ssec:templates}) and the
emission-line list (\S\ref{ssec:emlinelist}). We then describe the
three sequential algorithmic stages \fastspec{} executes in
turn---fast (preliminary) fitting in ``patches'' of wavelength
(\S\ref{ssec:patches}), stellar continuum modeling
(\S\ref{ssec:continuum}), and emission-line modeling
(\S\ref{ssec:emlines}). We close by describing the physical quantities
\fastspec{} derives from these fits (\S\ref{ssec:quantities}), how we
estimate their uncertainties (\S\ref{ssec:uncertainties}), and the
additional fitting modes, including a ``photometry-only'' mode called
\fastphot, built on this same machinery (\S\ref{sec:modes}).

\subsection{SPS Template Library}
\label{ssec:templates}

We generate SPS templates using Flexible Stellar Population
Synthesis\footnote{\url{https://github.com/moustakas/fsps}}
\citep[FSPS;][]{conroy09a,conroy10b} via a fork of the
\texttt{python-fsps}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/moustakas/python-fsps}}
interface. We construct these models using a custom version of the
\texttt{C3K} theoretical spectral library,
\texttt{C3K\_R3K}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/moustakas/c3k}},
the MIST stellar isochrones \citep{choi16a,dotter16a}, and a
\citet{chabrier03a} IMF. The \texttt{C3K\_R3K} library spans 100~\AA{}
to 1000~$\mu$m with $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{C3K}} \approx 7065$
($\lambda/\mathrm{FWHM}=3000$) in the rest-frame optical
(3500--9800~\AA) and constant-velocity $\Delta v=50$~\kms{} pixels. At
shorter and longer wavelengths our models have lower resolution and
sparser wavelength sampling, which keeps the UV-to-IR template
lightweight for synthetic photometry and dust-emission modeling
(\S\ref{sssec:fitting}) without wasting resolution where the
spectroscopic fit does not need it.

Following \citet{leja17a}, we build five ``continuity age'' basis
templates spanning fixed lookback-time bins, each assuming a constant
star-formation rate over its duration. The two youngest bins span
0--30~Myr and 30--100~Myr (with fixed widths of
$\Delta\log_{10}(t/\mathrm{yr}) = 7.48$ and $8.0$, respectively),
while the three oldest bins are logarithmically spaced between 100~Myr
and 13.7~Gyr with binning 0.1--1.1~Gyr, 1.1--11.6~Gyr, and
11.6--13.7~Gyr (see Figure~\ref{fig:sps}). We generate templates at
fixed solar metallicity, $\log_{10}(Z/Z_\odot) = 0$ (with no
$\alpha$-element enhancement), in two parallel variants---one
including and one excluding nebular emission (continuum and lines)
from the FSPS built-in photoionization grid \citep{byler17a}.

We index the resulting rest-frame templates $S_n(\lambda)$, $n =
1,\ldots,N$ (with $N=5$), by star-formation history (SFH) age bin and
nebular-emission variant, and normalize each to the flux density
produced at 10~pc by one solar mass of stars formed at the tabulated
lookback time,
\begin{equation}
  S_n(\lambda) =
  \frac{L_\odot}{4\pi(10\,\mathrm{pc})^2}\,\frac{L_{\lambda,n}}{L_\odot},
  \label{eq:fnorm}
\end{equation}
in units of
$\mathrm{erg\,s^{-1}\,cm^{-2}\,\text{\AA}^{-1}\,\msun^{-1}}$, so that
each template's fitted coefficient $c_n \geq 0$
(\S\ref{sssec:fitting}) carries units of \msun.

In addition, we generate a single infrared dust emission template
$S_\mathrm{dust}(\lambda)$ from \citet{draine07a}. This model is
parameterized by the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) mass
fraction, $q_\mathrm{PAH}$, the minimum starlight intensity heating
the diffuse ISM dust component $U_\mathrm{min}$, and the fraction
$\gamma$ of dust mass exposed to a power-law distribution of starlight
intensities (up to a fixed maximum $U_\mathrm{max}$) representing
photodissociation regions. We adopt fiducial values for our IR
template of $q_\mathrm{PAH} = 3.5$, $U_\mathrm{min} = 1.0$, and
$\gamma = 0.01$, and normalize it to unit bolometric luminosity.

accurate-enough pixel masks and kinematic initial guesses for the main
continuum and emission-line fits that follow (\S\ref{ssec:continuum},

We organize the 40 emission lines (\S\ref{ssec:emlinelist}) into 23
spectrally adjacent \emph{patches}, pre-assigned in the configuration
file \texttt{data/emlines.ecsv}. Each patch groups lines whose
wavelength windows overlap or nearly touch; for example, one patch
contains \hb\ together with [O\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda\lambda$4959,5007,
and another groups \ha\ with [N\,\textsc{ii}]~$\lambda\lambda$6548,6584.
We activate only patches with at least one line in the observed
spectral range for a given spectrum. Each line carries an \texttt{isstrong}
flag identifying the subset with typical fluxes reliable enough to
measure kinematics; the patch-fitting steps use only strong lines, while
the full list enters the final mask.

The algorithm proceeds in three steps. First, we fit all strong lines
with the narrow-only kinematic model (\S\ref{sssec:kinematic}), which
ties all forbidden lines to a single kinematic group anchored on
[O\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda$5007 and the narrow Balmer and
He\,\textsc{i} lines to a second group anchored on \ha. For each line,
we define a pixel window of $\pm2.5\,\sigma_\mathrm{line}$
centered on the expected observed-frame wavelength---with a minimum
floor of 50~\kms---and identify local continuum pixels from a
surrounding window that we iteratively expand until at least 11
unmasked pixels are available.  Within each patch we jointly fit line
amplitudes and a linear local continuum; we run two iterations,
updating the line-pixel windows with the best-fit velocity shifts and
widths before the second pass. Second, if any broad Balmer line falls
within the observed spectral range, we repeat the procedure with the
narrow+broad model, initializing the broad Balmer width at 1000~\kms;
if any broad Balmer line reaches $S/N > 1.5$ we adopt the broad-model
kinematics for masking, and otherwise retain the narrow-only
widths. Third, we rebuild the pixel mask over the full line list using
the converged kinematic parameters, widening the exclusion window to
$\pm5\,\sigma$ to conservatively exclude all line-contaminated pixels
from the continuum fit; we omit weak non-strong lines with $S/N < 3.5$
from the final mask to avoid over-masking. We additionally
mask pixels flagged as sky-line residuals and pixels with zero inverse
variance.

\subsection{Stellar Continuum Model}
\label{ssec:continuum}

This section describes the steps \fastspec{} uses to find the
best-fitting continuum model, $C(\lambda;\theta_C)$. Because speed and
flexibility are paramount, the default fitting mode uses a minimal
parameter set, $\theta_C = \{z,\mathbf{c},\tauv,\vdisp\}$, where $z$
is the redshift, $\mathbf{c}$ are the non-negative template
coefficients, $\tauv$ is the $V$-band dust optical depth, and $\vdisp$
is the stellar velocity dispersion. We emphasize, however, that the
fitting infrastructure can be easily extended to include additional
parameters (e.g., a two-component dust model or QSO model spectra),
which we discuss, in part, in \S\S \ref{sec:modes} and
\ref{sec:summary}.

For each object, we determine $\theta_C$ in three stages: we first
measure $\vdisp$ from stellar absorption features
(\S\ref{sssec:vdisp}); we then derive a scalar aperture correction
that places the DESI fiber spectrum on the total-flux scale of the
broadband photometry (\S\ref{sssec:apercorr}); and finally we jointly
fit for dust attenuation $\tauv$ and the template coefficients
$\mathbf{c}$, holding $z$ fixed at its input (Redrock) value
(\S\ref{sssec:fitting}). An optional nonparametric smooth-continuum
correction then removes residual low-level systematics
(\S\ref{sssec:smooth}) before the spectrum passes to the emission-line
fitter.

\subsubsection{Stellar Velocity Dispersion}
\label{sssec:vdisp}

The line-of-sight stellar velocity dispersion \vdisp{} traces the
depth of a galaxy's gravitational potential well; it is a key
observable for several galaxy scaling relations---the Fundamental
Plane, the black hole mass--\vdisp{} relation, and the
halo-mass--stellar-mass relation---and a fundamental ingredient for
most theoretical models of galaxy formation \citep[and references
  therein]{renzini06a, blanton09a, kormendy13a, vegetti24a}. A
reliable measurement, however, requires adequate rest-frame wavelength
coverage and relatively high signal-to-noise spectroscopy of the
absorption-line features most sensitive to dynamical broadening (e.g.,
Ca~H\&K, the $G$-band, H$\beta$, Mg~$b$). In DESI, these conditions
are generally met only for BGS and LRG targets; however, because
\fastspec{} does not use targeting information as a prior, we attempt
to measure \vdisp{} for every spectroscopic target.

We determine whether a direct measurement of \vdisp{} is possible from
two conditions: rest-frame wavelength coverage and ``fit
significance.'' First, we require a given target to have at least
1100~\AA{} of (unmasked) rest-frame wavelength coverage between 3800
and 4900~\AA, bridging Ca~H\&K, the 4000-\AA{} break, and H$\beta$. If
this condition is satisfied, then we perform the actual fit using all
unmasked pixels between 3800--6000~\AA{} (in the rest-frame). In
practice, this requirement means that we can only estimate \vdisp{}
for galaxies up to $z\approx1$. Second, we determine fit significance
by performing a fast $\chi^2$ grid scan of $N=6$ trial
velocity-broadening widths spanning $[50,\,500]$~\kms{},
simultaneously solving for \tauv{} and the template coefficients by
linear least-squares at each grid point (see
\S\ref{sssec:fitting}). If this scan yields a peak-to-peak
$\Delta\chi^2 \geq 25$ (and with the minimum value away from either
boundary) then we proceed with a direct measurement. In general,
$\Delta\chi^2 < 25$ implies that the spectrum has inadequate
signal-to-noise ratio in the stellar continuum (e.g., a faint ELG
target) to require \vdisp{} as an additional free parameter.

Most DESI targets in DR2 fail one or both of the preceding conditions;
however, we would still like to use an approximately correct value of
\vdisp. In this case, we estimate \vdisp{} from the
stellar-mass--velocity-dispersion relation,
\begin{equation}
  \log_{10}(\vdisp/\kms) = a + b\,(\logmstar - 11),
  \label{eq:sigma_mstar}
\end{equation}
with $(a,b) = (2.30,\,0.25)$ (\todo{ref?}) and using a fast,
preliminary estimate of the stellar mass, \Mstar{}
(\S\ref{sssec:fitting}). If our estimate of \Mstar{} fails---for
whatever reason---we instead adopt a fallback $\vdisp = 150$~\kms{}
(\texttt{VDISP\_NOMINAL}), close to the characteristic ``knee'' of the
velocity dispersion function \citep{bezanson11a, taylor22a}. In either
fallback case we set \texttt{VDISP\_IVAR}~$=0$ to flag that \vdisp{}
has not been independently measured.

When a direct measurement is warranted, we determine \vdisp{} by
finding the optimal Gaussian kernel dispersion,
$\vdisp^\mathrm{kern} \in [50,\,500]$~\kms{}, using
\texttt{scipy.optimize.least\_squares} with a Trust-Region Reflective
minimization algorithm \citep{branch99a}, fitting for
$\vdisp^\mathrm{kern}$ and \tauv{} simultaneously over the same range
as above. We then recover the physical velocity dispersion by adding
back the intrinsic resolution of our SPS models in quadrature,
\begin{equation}
  \vdisp = \sqrt{(\vdisp^\mathrm{kern})^2 + \sigma_\mathrm{C3K}^2},
  \label{eq:vdisptot}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_\mathrm{C3K} = c/\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{C3K}} \approx
42$~\kms{} (\S\ref{ssec:templates}).

\subsubsection{Aperture Correction}
\label{sssec:apercorr}

By default, \fastspec{} fits the spectrum ($0.36-0.98$\,\micron) of
each extragalactic DESI target simultaneously with its corresponding
seven-band ($grz$W1--W4) Legacy Surveys integrated photometry
($0.47-22$\,\micron; \S\ref{ssec:photometry}). Fitting all the
available spectrophotometry over the broadest possible wavelength
baseline helps constrain physical properties like dust attenuation,
and ties the derived stellar masses, star-formation rates, and
rest-frame photometry to the total light of each source rather than to
the light captured by the $1\farcs5$-diameter DESI fiber. However, we
have to account for the fact that the spectrum and photometry are
measured over different spatial scales before combining them.

So-called aperture effects (or aperture bias) are extremely difficult
to treat in detail because galaxies exhibit genuine radial gradients
(or, more generally, spatial inhomogeneities) in color, stellar
populations, age, metallicity, and more \citep[and references
  therein]{sanchez20a}. In addition, the amount of light the DESI
fiber captures depends on each galaxy's apparent (angular) size on the
sky, and therefore on redshift through the angular diameter distance
\citep{hogg99a}. These effects conspire to make aperture bias a strong
function of target class, affecting low-redshift BGS targets the most
and higher-redshift, angularly compact, and more
spectrophotometrically homogeneous LRG and ELG targets the least.

Nevertheless, in the current version of \fastspec{} we use the
simplest possible model to account for aperture bias: we assume that
the spectral shape within the fiber is representative of the galaxy as
a whole, independent of wavelength or the magnitude of the aperture
correction. We derive this correction by synthesizing $grz$ photometry
from a fast initial fit to the DESI spectrum to get $C_f^\mathrm{fib}$
with $f \in \{g,\,r,\,z\}$, and computing the per-band multiplicative
factor
\begin{equation}
  \eta_f = \frac{D_f}{C_f^\mathrm{fib}},
  \label{eq:apercorr}
\end{equation}
where $D_f$ is the total Tractor flux of the object in each
bandpass. We then scale the DESI spectrum by the median value,
$\bar{\eta}=\mathrm{median}(\eta_g,\,\eta_r,\,\eta_z)$ during joint
spectrophotometric fitting (\S\ref{sssec:fitting}). \todo{Do we need

\subsubsection{Fitting Algorithm}
\label{sssec:fitting}

To fit the stellar continuum we use a fully forward-modeling approach:
we carry each rest-frame SPS template (\S\ref{ssec:templates}) through
a sequence of physical and instrumental transformations---dust
attenuation, redshifting, and instrumental broadening---into a
prediction that can be compared directly to the observed
spectrophotometry, and then solve for the template weights and dust
attenuation (at fixed \vdisp; see \S\ref{sssec:vdisp}) that minimize
the inverse-variance-weighted residuals.

First, we model the attenuation of each template as a
uniform foreground screen,
\begin{equation}
  S_n^\mathrm{att}(\lambda) = S_n(\lambda)\,e^{-\tauv k(\lambda)},
  \label{eq:atten}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $S_n(\lambda)$ is the unattenuated template given by
equation~(\ref{eq:fnorm}), $\tauv$ is the $V$-band optical depth, and

\begin{equation}
  k(\lambda) =
  \left(\frac{\lambda}{5500\,\text{\AA}}\right)^{n}
  \label{eq:kl}
\end{equation}

\noindent is the attenuation curve, which we model as a simple power
law with fixed power-law index $n=-0.7$ based on the
\citet{charlot00a} birth-cloud model.  Absorbed photons are re-emitted
in the IR following energy balance: we scale the \citet{draine07a}
dust emission template from \S\ref{ssec:templates},
$S_\mathrm{dust}(\lambda)$, by the luminosity absorbed from each SPS
template,
\begin{equation}
  L_{\mathrm{abs},n} = \int_0^\infty S_n(\lambda) (1 - e^{-\tauv
    k(\lambda)})\,d\lambda,
  \label{eq:labs}
\end{equation}
so that the total (attenuated plus reradiated) continuum template
conserves bolometric luminosity,
\begin{equation}
  S_n^\mathrm{tot}(\lambda) = S_n^\mathrm{att}(\lambda) +
  L_{\mathrm{abs},n}\,S_\mathrm{dust}(\lambda).
  \label{eq:ftot}
\end{equation}
We use a single dust emission template with default parameters
$q_\mathrm{PAH}=3.5$, $U_\mathrm{min}=1.0$, and $\gamma=0.01$ both for
speed and because most DESI targets do not have adequately deep IR
photometry to constrain a larger number of dust emission
parameters.

For an object at redshift $z$ with luminosity distance $D_L$ (in Mpc),
we relate each rest-frame continuum template to its observed-frame
counterpart,
\begin{equation}
  S_n^\mathrm{obs}(\lambda)
  =
  T_\mathrm{IGM}(\lambda,z)\,
  \frac{\mathcal{F}_0\,\mathcal{M}_0}
       {4\pi\,(3.086\times10^{24}\,D_L)^2}
  \,\frac{S_n^\mathrm{tot}(\lambda/(1+z))}{1+z},
  \label{eq:zfactor}
\end{equation}
where $T_\mathrm{IGM}(\lambda,z)$ is the \citet{inoue14a}
intergalactic medium (IGM) transmission function, accounting for
Ly$\alpha$ forest and Lyman-limit attenuation; $\mathcal{F}_0 =
10^{17}$ is a dimensionless scale factor converting the cgs flux
densities of \S\ref{ssec:templates} to our internal flux units of
$10^{-17}$~erg~s$^{-1}$~cm$^{-2}$~\AA$^{-1}$; and $\mathcal{M}_0 =
10^{10}\,\msun$ is a fixed mass normalization that rescales the fitted
template coefficients to order unity for a well-conditioned
non-negative least-squares (NNLS) solve (see below).

%We combine the scalar prefactor and IGM transmission into a single
%array (\texttt{zfactors}) evaluated once per object at initialization
%and reused for all template evaluations during fitting.

We restrict the dust optical depth to the physically motivated range
$\tauv \in [0,\,2]$, and by default include coefficients for all five
SPS age bins (\S\ref{ssec:templates}), even for stellar populations
nominally older than the age of the universe at the redshift of a
given object. Because our age sampling is coarse, the boundary between
physical and unphysical ages is poorly defined, and excluding bins
outright would introduce more redshift-dependent bias than it removes
\citep{leja19a}.

We fit the stellar continuum by minimizing the
inverse-variance-weighted sum of squared spectroscopic and photometric
residuals between the data and the continuum model
$C(\lambda;\theta_C)$,
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2(\tauv,\vdisp,\mathbf{c})
  =
  \sum_i I_i\bigl(d_i - C_i\bigr)^2
  \;+\;
  \sum_k I_{D,k}\bigl(D_k - C_k\bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:chi2cont}
\end{equation}
where the first sum runs over unmasked spectral pixels with data $d_i$
and inverse variance $I_i$, the second runs over photometric bands with
data $D_k$ and inverse variance $I_{D,k} > 0$, and $C_i \equiv
C(\lambda_i;\theta_C)$ and $C_k$ denote the continuum model evaluated
at each spectral pixel and synthesized through each bandpass,
respectively; $d_i$ already carries the aperture correction from
\S\ref{sssec:apercorr}, so no additional scaling factor appears here.
When $\vdisp$ is fixed, the continuum model is linear in
$\mathbf{c}$ for any fixed $\tauv$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{C} = \boldsymbol{\Psi}\,\mathbf{c},
  \label{eq:designmatrix}
\end{equation}
where each column of the design matrix $\boldsymbol{\Psi}$ is one
fully transformed template $S_n^\mathrm{obs}$---attenuated and
redshifted as above, broadened by $\vdisp$
(\S\ref{sssec:vdisp}), and convolved with the wavelength-dependent
DESI resolution matrix (\S\ref{sssec:emline_model})---evaluated at the
spectral pixels and synthesized through the photometric bandpasses,
and weighted by $\sqrt{I_i}$ or $\sqrt{I_{D,k}}$ as appropriate.

This structure is linear in the many template coefficients
$\mathbf{c}$ but nonlinear only in the single scalar $\tauv$, so
rather than search all free parameters simultaneously we exploit the
separability via variable projection (VARPRO;
\citealt{golub73a}): an outer Brent scalar minimization over $\tauv$
(typically 10--15 evaluations) with an inner NNLS solve for
$\mathbf{c}$ at each trial $\tauv$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{c}\,\geq\,0}
  \bigl\|\boldsymbol{\Psi}\,\mathbf{c} - \mathbf{b}\bigr\|^2,
  \label{eq:nnls}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{b}$ is the corresponding $\sqrt{I}$-weighted vector of
stacked spectroscopic and photometric data. Because the inner NNLS
solve is exact at every trial $\tauv$, VARPRO converges $\sim10\times$
faster than direct optimization over the full parameter vector, which
would otherwise require finite-differencing across all template
coefficients. When $\vdisp$ is a free parameter, the separability is
broken and we instead fit all parameters jointly with
\texttt{scipy.optimize.least\_squares} using the TRF method.

\subsubsection{Smooth Continuum Correction}
\label{sssec:smooth}

The parametric continuum model $C(\lambda)$ may leave low-level
systematic residuals due to imperfect template matching or
flux-calibration uncertainties \citep[e.g.,][]{tremonti04a}. We
estimate and remove these with a nonparametric smooth continuum
computed per camera from the spectrum after subtracting the best-fit,
line-free continuum model. Working per camera, we slide a 75-pixel
window in 125-pixel steps across the residual spectrum, applying a
$2\sigma$ iterative clip in each window and computing the robust
median and inter-quartile sigma; we discard windows with fewer than 15
unmasked pixels. We then fit a weighted quadratic spline
(\texttt{UnivariateSpline}, $k = 2$) to the window medians and add the
resulting smooth baseline back to $C(\lambda)$ before emission-line
fitting, so that the line fitter operates on spectra with an accurate

\subsection{Emission-Line Model}
\label{ssec:emlines}

Having fit the continuum model $C(\lambda;\theta_C)$
(\S\ref{ssec:continuum}), we model the continuum-subtracted residual
spectrum with the emission-line model $E(\lambda;\theta_E)$ introduced
in equation~\eqref{eq:totmodel}: a forward model built from the
log-Gaussian profiles of individual lines, coupled to the DESI
resolution matrix (\S\ref{sssec:emline_model}). We first describe how
we group lines by shared kinematics (this section), then the
forward model itself (\S\ref{sssec:emline_model}), and finally how we
decide between narrow-only and narrow+broad kinematic models
(\S\ref{sssec:kinematic}).

\subsubsection{Forward Model}
\label{sssec:emline_model}

After subtracting the best-fitting continuum model $C(\lambda)$ from
the data $d_i$ (\S~\ref{sssec:fitting}), including the smooth
continuum correction (\S~\ref{sssec:smooth}), we fit the residual
spectrum $r_i \equiv d_i - C_i$ using a forward model assuming
log-Gaussian emission-line profiles.

We model the intrinsic spectral profile of each emission line as a
log-Gaussian in observed wavelength,
\begin{equation}
  \phi(\lambda;\,A,\lambda^*,\sigma)
  =
  A\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\ln\lambda - \ln\lambda^*)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),
  \label{eq:intrinsic}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the peak amplitude (in \fluxdensity), $\lambda^*$ is the
observed line center, including both cosmological and peculiar motion
of the line-emitting gas,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^* = \lambda_\mathrm{rest}
    \left(1 + z + \frac{\Delta v}{c}\right),
  \label{eq:lstar}
\end{equation}
and $\sigma = s/c$ is the line width in log-$\lambda$ units. With this
parameterization, the total line flux in \flux{} is
\begin{equation}
  \Phi = \sqrt{2\pi}\,A\,\lambda^*\,\sigma
       = \sqrt{2\pi}\,A\,\lambda^*\,\frac{s}{c},
  \label{eq:flux_phys}
\end{equation}
which is independent of the instrumental pixel grid and resolution
matrix.

\fastspecfit{} takes advantage of the fact that DESI spectra are
produced by an optimal extraction that encodes the
wavelength-dependent instrumental resolution in a sparse, band-format
resolution matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (with $\sim$11 diagonals), normalized
to preserve integrated flux. The standard discrete model $\mathbf{m}
\approx \mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\phi}$ (with $\boldsymbol{\phi}$
evaluated at pixel centers) fails for features narrower than
approximately one pixel. A more accurate discretization uses a
Gaussian-pre-convolved spectrum evaluated at pixel centers: defining
$\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}$, where $\mathbf{M}_{ij} =
G(\lambda_i - \lambda_j;\,0,\,\sigma_G)$ is a Gaussian design matrix
with width $\sigma_G = 0.5$\,\AA, the predicted line model is
$\mathbf{m} \approx \mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{h}$, where $\mathbf{h}$ is the
$\sigma_G$-pre-convolved profile evaluated at pixel centers
\citep[following][]{koposov25a}.

Because the log-Gaussian profile~\eqref{eq:intrinsic} is an exact
Gaussian in log-$\lambda$ space, the convolution with $G_{\sigma_G}$ is
analytic. Defining $x_j = \ln\lambda_j - \ln\lambda^*$ and the
effective width $\sigma_\mathrm{eff} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + \sigma_{G,\ell}^2}$
(where $\sigma_{G,\ell} = \sigma_G/\lambda^*$), the pre-convolved profile
of a single line at pixel center $\lambda_j$ is
\begin{equation}
  F_j = A\,\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_\mathrm{eff}}
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{x_j^2}{2\sigma_\mathrm{eff}^2}\right).
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
The amplitude factor $\sigma/\sigma_\mathrm{eff} \leq 1$ accounts for
broadening by the pre-convolution kernel. The full emission-line model
is $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{W}\,\mathbf{F}$, summed over all $N$ emission
lines falling within the observed spectral range. Physical constraints
(shared $\Delta v$ and $s$ within kinematic groups; fixed doublet
amplitude ratios) reduce the free-parameter count and improve
reliability for blended or weak lines.

One of the advantages of this implementation is that we can supply
\texttt{scipy.optimize.least\_squares} with an analytic
Jacobian--closed-form first derivatives of $F_j$
(equation~\ref{eq:model}) with respect to each free parameter. Because
$F_j$ is linear in $A$, the amplitude gradient is simply
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial A} = \frac{F_j}{A}.
  \label{eq:dF_dA}
\end{equation}
The velocity-shift gradient follows from the dependence of
$\ln\lambda^* = \ln[\lambda_\mathrm{rest}(1 + z + \Delta v/c)]$ on
$\Delta v$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial \Delta v}
  =
  \frac{F_j\,x_j}{\sigma_\mathrm{eff}^2\,c\,(1+z+\Delta v/c)}.
  \label{eq:dF_dv}
\end{equation}
Using $1/c$ in place of $1/[c\,(1+z+\Delta v/c)]$ would introduce a
fractional error of order $z$ at all redshifts and must be avoided. The
line-width gradient accounts for the dependence of both the amplitude
factor $\sigma/\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ and the exponent on $\sigma = s/c$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial F_j}{\partial s}
  =
  \frac{A}{\sigma_\mathrm{eff}\,c}\,
  e^{-x_j^2/(2\sigma_\mathrm{eff}^2)}\,
  \Bigl[1 - \sigma^2\bigl(1 - x_j^2/\sigma_\mathrm{eff}^2
  \bigr)/\sigma_\mathrm{eff}^2\Bigr].
  \label{eq:dF_ds}
\end{equation}

We minimize

  \chi^2 = \sum_{j} I_{j}\bigl(r_j - E_j\bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:chi2line}
\end{equation}
where $r_j \equiv d_j - C_j$ is the continuum-subtracted observed flux

algorithm
\citep[\texttt{scipy.optimize.least\_squares};][]{branch99a}.  We
compute line fluxes analytically from the fitted $(A,\,\lambda^*,\,s)$

sampling or the resolution matrix.

\subsubsection{Kinematic Groups and Broad-Line Decomposition}
\label{sssec:kinematic}

We reduce the dimensionality of the emission-line fit by grouping lines
into \emph{kinematic groups}, within which all members share a common
velocity shift $\Delta v$ and line width $s$. Eight UV and broad AGN
permitted lines carry independently free kinematics in both fitting
models: Ly$\alpha$, [N\,\textsc{v}]~$\lambda$1240,
O\,\textsc{i}~$\lambda$1304, Si\,\textsc{iv}~$\lambda$1396,
C\,\textsc{iv}~$\lambda$1549, He\,\textsc{ii}~$\lambda$1640,
Al\,\textsc{iii}~$\lambda$1857, and He\,\textsc{ii}~$\lambda$4686.
We treat two doublet pairs as global kinematic anchors in both
models: Si\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda$1892 is tied to
C\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda$1908, and Mg\,\textsc{ii}~$\lambda\lambda$2796,2803
shares a single kinematic solution with a free amplitude ratio.

\input{tables/table-kinematic-bounds}

We fit two profile models whose kinematic structure depends on whether a
significant broad Balmer component is present. The \emph{narrow-only}
model (\texttt{linemodel\_nobroad}) places all forbidden lines in a
single group anchored to [O\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda$5007, groups the
narrow Balmer series together with He\,\textsc{i} lines in a second
group anchored to H$\alpha$, and fixes all broad Balmer amplitudes to
zero. The \emph{narrow+broad} model (\texttt{linemodel\_broad})
collapses all narrow lines---Balmer, He\,\textsc{i}, and
forbidden---into a single group anchored to narrow H$\alpha$, retains a
separate kinematic group for [O\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda\lambda$4959,5007,
and adds a third group for broad Balmer emission anchored to broad
H$\alpha$. Narrow-line bounds are $1 < s < 750$~\kms\ and
$|\Delta v| < 500$~\kms; broad-line bounds are
$1 < s < 10{,}000$~\kms\ and $|\Delta v| < 2{,}500$~\kms. We load all
kinematic constraints, amplitude ratios, and optimizer bounds at startup
from the bundled configuration file
\texttt{data/emline-constraints.yaml}.

Several line pairs carry additional constraints on their relative
amplitudes. We fix three doublet ratios to theoretical atomic
emissivities: [N\,\textsc{ii}]~$\lambda$6548/$\lambda$6584 $= 1/3.033$,
[O\,\textsc{iii}]~$\lambda$4959/$\lambda$5007 $= 1/2.964$, and
[O\,\textsc{ii}]~$\lambda$7330/$\lambda$7320 $= 1/1.225$. Three
density-sensitive doublets---[O\,\textsc{ii}]~$\lambda\lambda$3726,3729;
[S\,\textsc{ii}]~$\lambda\lambda$6716,6731; and
Mg\,\textsc{ii}~$\lambda\lambda$2796,2803---carry free amplitude ratios
that encode the electron density.

After the primary constrained fit, \fastspecfit{} runs a second
optimization pass that frees individual velocity shifts for each
narrow line, allowing it to absorb small wavelength-calibration
residuals; line widths remain shared within each kinematic group.
Doublet-constrained pairs retain their kinematic ties during the relaxed
pass, since releasing transitions from the same upper level individually
would allow unphysical offsets between their velocity shifts. We adopt
the relaxed solution only when the global $\chi^2$ improvement exceeds
the number of additional free parameters; otherwise we retain the
primary result.

\fastspecfit{} fits every spectrum with both the narrow-only and
narrow+broad models simultaneously, then selects the preferred solution
based on four conditions that must all be satisfied. First, the
reduction in $\chi^2$ over the pixels spanning the broad Balmer lines
must exceed the corresponding reduction in degrees of freedom,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\chi^2_\mathrm{Balmer} \;>\; \Delta N_\mathrm{dof,\,Balmer},
  \label{eq:broadchi2}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\chi^2 = \chi^2_\mathrm{narrow} - \chi^2_\mathrm{broad}$
and $\Delta N_\mathrm{dof}$ counts the additional free parameters
introduced by the broad-Balmer group, evaluated only over pixels within
$\pm N_\sigma$ of the non-helium broad Balmer lines. Second, the fitted
velocity dispersion of the broad \ha\ component must exceed
$\sigma_\mathrm{broad}(\ha) > 250$~\kms, excluding spurious broad fits
driven by noise or continuum residuals. Third, the broad component must
be wider than the narrow component,
$\sigma_\mathrm{broad}(\ha) > \sigma_\mathrm{narrow}(\ha)$, ensuring a
physical hierarchy between the two kinematic components. Fourth, at
least one of the two brightest broad Balmer lines in the spectral range
must satisfy $S/N_\mathrm{broad} > 2.5$, where we estimate the noise
robustly from the interquartile range of the fit residuals in the
Balmer-line pixels after subtracting the full broad model. If any
condition fails, we retain the narrow-only result. We record the
pixel-level $\chi^2$ comparison in the output columns
\texttt{DELTA\_LINECHI2} and \texttt{DELTA\_LINENDOF}.

After the broad/narrow model selection, \fastspecfit{} runs an optional
kinematic relaxation pass. For the narrow-only model, we free
individual velocity shifts within a $\pm100$~\kms\ window around each
line's primary best-fit value, while line widths remain tied; this
configuration accommodates galaxies with forbidden-line outflows, where
the forbidden and Balmer lines often settle at slightly different
systemic velocities. For the narrow+broad model, we do not relax any
kinematic parameters, because the three-group structure already
absorbs most residual complexity through the broad Balmer component,
and freeing kinematics in that model risks over-fitting. In both cases we adopt the relaxed
solution only when the $\chi^2$ improvement weighted by the degrees of
freedom exceeds the degrees of freedom added by the relaxation;
otherwise we retain the primary result. We record this comparison in the
output columns \texttt{DELTA\_KINECHI2} and \texttt{DELTA\_KINENDOF}.

\subsubsection{Emission-Line Quantities}
\label{sssec:lines}

We compute equivalent widths (EWs) for all modeled emission lines as the ratio
of the integrated line flux to the local continuum flux density, which we
interpolate from $C(\lambda)$; this choice ensures that EW estimates remain
insensitive to continuum-subtraction noise in the observed spectrum. We measure
the $4000$~\AA\ break strength $D_n(4000)$ \citep{balogh99a} in two
complementary ways: from $C(\lambda)$ alone, which is noise-free but sensitive
to template mismatch, and directly from the observed spectrum after subtracting
the best-fitting emission-line model $E(\lambda)$, which is noisy but
model-independent. We report both estimates, which together provide a
consistency check on the stellar population fit.

We flag a line as a significant detection when $A\,\sqrt{I_A} > 1.5$,
where $I_A$ is the inverse variance of the observed flux density at
the line center; we report lines falling below this threshold as
$1\sigma$ upper limits, with continuum flux densities interpolated
from the best-fitting continuum model $C(\lambda)$. Equivalent widths
use the same continuum-model-interpolated continuum.

\subsection{Spectrophotometric and Physical Properties}
\label{ssec:quantities}

We derive several physical quantities from the best-fitting continuum
model and report them in the output catalog
(\S\ref{sec:appendix_datamodel}). We compute the stellar mass as the
sum of the NNLS template coefficients $\mathbf{c}$, which we weight by
the FSPS surviving-mass fractions that account for the mass stellar
evolution returns to the ISM via winds and supernovae. We caution that
non-parametric SFH models can yield stellar masses that are
systematically higher---by $\sim$0.2--0.3~dex on average---than
parametric estimates, because a flexible SFH can accommodate more mass
in old, low-luminosity stellar populations \citep{leja19a,leja20a}. We
estimate the instantaneous star-formation rate (SFR) from the
coefficient of the youngest age bin (0--100~Myr), dividing by the
width of that bin to obtain the mean SFR averaged over the most recent
30~Myr of star-formation history. Although \fastspecfit{} measures
both the \ha{} luminosity and the rest-frame UV continuum---each of
which serves as an independent SFR diagnostic---we do not currently
convert either quantity to a SFR within the code.

We compute K-corrections by comparing synthesized observer-frame and rest-frame
photometry from the best-fitting continuum model $C(\lambda)$
\citep{hogg02a,blanton07a}, and we derive rest-frame absolute magnitudes in each
Legacy Survey band via the standard distance-modulus relation. We additionally
report monochromatic luminosities at 1450, 1500, 1700, 2800, 3000, and 5100~\AA,
which we interpolate from $C(\lambda)$ and which are especially useful for
UV-selected samples and AGN host-galaxy studies.

\subsection{Parameter Uncertainties}
\label{ssec:uncertainties}

We estimate parameter uncertainties by repeating the continuum fit on
$N_\mathrm{monte} = 50$ independent Gaussian realizations of the
observed spectra and photometry,
\begin{equation}
  d_i^{(m)} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(d_i,\,I_i^{-1}\bigr),
  \qquad m = 1,\ldots,N_\mathrm{monte},
\end{equation}
drawn with a fixed random seed and reused for both the continuum and
emission-line fits. The inverse variance of each fitted quantity
$\theta$ is then
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_\theta^{-2}
  = \bigl[\mathrm{Var}(\theta^{(1)},\ldots,\theta^{(N_\mathrm{monte})})\bigr]^{-1}.
  \label{eq:mc_unc}
\end{equation}
We report Monte Carlo uncertainties for \tauv, \vdisp\ (when fitted),
$D_n(4000)$, rest-frame luminosities, and observed-frame continuum flux
densities.

\subsection{Updated QSO Redshifts}
\label{ssec:zqso}

For a small but astrophysically important fraction of quasar targets, the
\texttt{Redrock} spectral classification pipeline assigns an incorrect redshift.
We correct these using the \texttt{QuasarNet} and MgII afterburner catalogs
distributed alongside each \texttt{Redrock} output file. For primary QSO
targets, we replace the \texttt{Redrock} redshift when two conditions are met:
\texttt{QuasarNet} independently classifies the object as a QSO, and the maximum
line confidence across six diagnostic transitions (Ly$\alpha$, C\,\textsc{iv},
C\,\textsc{iii}], Mg\,\textsc{ii}, \hb, and \ha) exceeds 0.99 for DR2 (0.95 for
DR1 and the EDR). A broader criterion is applied to variable WISE QSO secondary
targets; full details are documented in the online VAC documentation. The
original \texttt{Redrock} redshift and \texttt{ZWARN} bitmask are preserved in
the output columns \texttt{Z\_RR} and \texttt{ZWARN\_RR}. In total, corrected
redshifts are applied to approximately 9,100 targets in the EDR, 74,000 in DR1,
and 169,000 in DR2.

\section{DR2 VAC Data Model}
\label{sec:appendix_datamodel}

The \fastspecfit{} output is stored in a compressed FITS file
named \texttt{fastspec-\textit{SURVEY}-\textit{PROGRAM}-\textit{HEALPIX}.fits.gz},
where \textit{SURVEY} (e.g., \texttt{main}), \textit{PROGRAM}
(e.g., \texttt{bright} or \texttt{dark}), and \textit{HEALPIX} identify
the observing program and sky region. Each file contains five
extensions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{HDU0} (\texttt{PRIMARY}): empty image with processing
    metadata keywords, including the software version, Monte Carlo
    random seed, and boolean fitting flags.
  \item \texttt{HDU1} (\texttt{METADATA}): target metadata mirroring
    the DESI redshift catalog, including sky coordinates, the input
    redshift and zwarning bitmask, targeting bits, and extinction-corrected
    Legacy Survey photometry.
  \item \texttt{HDU2} (\texttt{SPECPHOT}): derived spectrophotometric
    quantities, including SFH template coefficients, stellar-population
    parameters ($\tau_V$, $\vdisp$, age, metallicity, $\logmstar$, SFR),
    $D_n(4000)$, synthesized photometry, K-corrections, absolute
    magnitudes, monochromatic luminosities, and continuum flux densities
    at key emission-line centers.
  \item \texttt{HDU3} (\texttt{FASTSPEC}): emission-line fitting results,
    including per-camera median S/N and smooth continuum corrections,
    aperture correction factors, doublet line ratios, and per-line
    measurements (amplitude, flux, equivalent width, velocity shift,
    line width, and upper limits) for all modeled emission lines.
  \item \texttt{HDU4} (\texttt{MODELS}): 3D image array
    [$N_\lambda \times 3 \times N_\mathrm{obj}$] with the best-fitting
    model spectra on a common linear wavelength grid (3600--9824\,\AA,
    $0.8$\,\AA\,pixel$^{-1}$, vacuum wavelengths). The three planes
    store the full spectrophotometric model, the smooth continuum
    correction alone, and the emission-line model alone, respectively.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{table:datamodel} describes the key output columns in the
\texttt{SPECPHOT} and \texttt{FASTSPEC} extensions. All integrated
line fluxes are in units of \flux, flux densities in \fluxdensity, and
uncertainties are stored as inverse variances (a value of zero indicates
the parameter was not measured or the uncertainty is undefined).
A machine-readable description of the complete data model is maintained
in the \fastspecfit{} online
documentation.\footnote{\url{https://fastspecfit.readthedocs.io/en/latest/fastspec.html}}

\section{Pipeline Diagnostics}
\label{sec:appendix_diagnostics}

One of the dividends of fitting every DESI spectrum with a physically
motivated forward model is that the residuals carry information about
the quality of the underlying data reduction. In this appendix we use
\fastspecfit{} measurements to characterize two classes of residual
systematic error in the DR2 pipeline: wavelength-solution errors
revealed by per-camera emission-line velocity shifts
(\S\ref{ssec:waveresid}), and spectrophotometric calibration residuals
revealed by the difference between synthesized and observed broadband
photometry as a function of magnitude, redshift, and target class
(\S\ref{ssec:fluxresid}).

\subsection{Wavelength Calibration Residuals}
\label{ssec:waveresid}

\fastspecfit{} fits each of the three DESI cameras (B, R, Z) separately
and records the median emission-line velocity shift per camera for every
spectrum in which emission lines are detected. For a perfectly calibrated
wavelength solution, this per-camera shift should be consistent with zero
and with the global redshift measured by Redrock. Systematic offsets or
trends with wavelength, fiber position, or observing conditions indicate
residual errors in the arc-lamp or sky-line wavelength calibration.

\section{EDR and DR1 Value-Added Catalogs}
\label{sec:appendix_vacs}

Two known issues affect the v1.0 DR2 catalog. First, the
inverse-variance estimates for emission-line fluxes and equivalent
widths carry significant errors; users requiring precise flux
uncertainties should apply appropriate caution and consult the online
documentation for the latest status of corrections. Second, a small
fraction of spectra with strong emission lines suffer poor line fits
due to a clipping error in the internal flux uncertainty routine. We
fix both issues in \fastspecfit{}~v3.2.0, and we will release updated
catalogs in due course.
